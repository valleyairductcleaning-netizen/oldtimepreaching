Exoplanet Review: Apparently Man Can Conquer the Universe, but Nobody Can Conquer the Foul Language

An entertaining science-fiction adventure, excellent sound effects, and another reminder that mankind apparently doesn't need God—as long as somebody remembers to bring a calculator.

As with my other Christian book reviews, I am interested in more than whether a story keeps me entertained. Proverbs 3:5 warns against leaning on our own understanding. That matters when a story puts so much confidence in human ingenuity.

Audiobook: Exoplanet
Written by: Benjamin Percy
Executive Producer: Andy Weir
Format: Full-cast Audible Original
Rating: I haven't figured out how to rate this one.
Christian Recommendation: Save your time. There are better things to listen to.

Length: 4 hours, 8 minutes.

View Exoplanet on Audible Canada

Exoplanet Audible Review: A Sci-Fi Thriller with a Full Cast

Exoplanet is a sci-fi thriller written by Benjamin Percy and executive produced by Andy Weir. This Audible Originals production brings together Anthony Mackie, Ariana DeBose, John Leguizamo, and Ray Porter as part of its full cast. That is a considerable amount of talent to squeeze into four hours. Apparently, there was room for all those actors, but not quite enough room to let the story breathe.

The adventure combines space survival, time travel, and a threatening alien intelligence. A disaster separates the crew across different eras on the same dangerous planet. That is exactly the sort of mystery that kept me listening—and exactly why I wanted more time spent developing it.

Audible positions the production for listeners who enjoy Project Hail Mary, Red Rising, The Expanse, and Dark Matter. I came to it after Project Hail Mary, with familiar frustrations from The Martian still in mind. Those comparisons explain the audience it is aimed at; they are not recommendations from me for every title on that list.

Well, Here We Go Again

I have a confession to make.

I actually enjoyed listening to Exoplanet.

There, I said it. Hopefully, nobody revokes my membership in the Reformed community.

After listening to Project Hail Mary, I figured I would give this audiobook a chance. After all, Andy Weir was involved, and despite my rather serious disagreements with his worldview, the man certainly knows how to be involved in an interesting science-fiction adventure.

So I pressed play.

And wouldn't you know it? The story was actually pretty good.

It kept me interested, wondering what was coming next, and occasionally sitting on the edge of my seat.

Of course, it wasn't long before I was reminded why I have such a difficult time with these books.

Apparently, you can travel across the universe, confront extraordinary dangers, and solve problems that would leave the world's greatest scientists scratching their heads.

But finding a vocabulary that doesn't require constant profanity?

Now that's apparently beyond human achievement.

The Sound Effects Were Excellent. Somebody Give These People a Christian History Book!

Let me give credit where credit is due.

The acting was excellent. The sound effects were excellent. The entire production was exceptionally well done.

Skywalker Sound handled the sound design, and the production is available in Dolby Atmos on Audible. I am not claiming I tested every audio setup, but the acting and sound effects I heard were excellent. Somebody clearly knew what they were doing behind the mixing desk.

This wasn't one of those audiobooks where a fellow reads for four hours while you wonder whether he fell asleep halfway through chapter three.

No, this was a proper audio drama.

Different actors, realistic sounds, dramatic scenes, and enough atmosphere to make you feel as though you were right there.

I genuinely enjoyed it.

And that got me thinking.

Why on earth aren't Christian publishers doing more of this?

We have two thousand years of extraordinary Christian history.

Missionaries travelling into dangerous lands. Reformers standing against powerful religious authorities. Christians being imprisoned for their faith. Men and women risking everything to proclaim the Gospel.

Real danger. Real courage. Real suffering.

And, unlike science fiction, these things actually happened!

Imagine the life of John Paton presented as a full-cast audio drama.

Or the experiences of John Bunyan.

Or the life of William Tyndale.

We could have excellent acting, remarkable sound effects, and stories that actually point people toward the providence of God.

But no.

Apparently, we're going to spend our production budgets telling another story about how mankind can save the universe with a screwdriver, three equations, and a cheerful disregard for his Creator.

Four Hours? Was Somebody Charging the Author by the Minute?

Now, one of my biggest disappointments was the length of the story.

Just over four hours.

Four hours!

There was enough potential here for an eight-hour production, perhaps even twelve.

I wanted more survival. More mystery. More danger. More opportunity to explore what was happening.

Instead, the story sometimes felt as though somebody was standing behind the author shouting, "Hurry up! We've only got twenty minutes left!"

There were gaps in the storytelling, and I felt the whole adventure needed more room to breathe.

And then there was the ending.

Ah, yes. The science-fiction ending.

Apparently, science-fiction authors are excellent at getting their characters into impossible situations but sometimes struggle to bring everything to a satisfying conclusion.

They can create an entire universe.

They just seem to have trouble figuring out when and how to finish the story.

Now, I cannot tell you exactly how I would have ended Exoplanet differently. But I would have liked a more developed adventure leading up to it.

If the story had been eight or twelve hours long, I doubt it would have needed a sequel.

The Characters: Not Everybody Needs to Be Captain of the Universe

The male characters were generally good.

The female characters? Well, let's just say they weren't my favourites.

I wasn't particularly impressed with the female characters being placed in certain leadership roles, such as captain and pilot, especially when they were written with personalities I found unnecessarily masculine.

I realize that's not going to win me an invitation to speak at Hollywood's next diversity conference.

I'll try to recover from the disappointment.

But these are my personal preferences, and I didn't find those character portrayals appealing.

It didn't ruin the entire adventure, but it certainly didn't improve it for me.

Apparently the Universe Has Been Saved, but the English Language Is Beyond Repair

Now we arrive at the part that really irritated me.

The language.

Good grief.

Why?

Why must an otherwise interesting story be filled with unnecessary foul language?

Is there some unwritten rule in modern science fiction that every intelligent character must demonstrate his intelligence by sounding like a sailor who just hit his thumb with a hammer?

I genuinely don't understand it.

You have an interesting story.

You have talented actors.

You have excellent sound effects.

You have enough imagination to build an entire fictional universe.

And somehow, despite all that talent, you still can't find an alternative to profanity?

Really?

This was one of my biggest frustrations with The Martian.

That was another good story, but the language was so excessive that I began to wonder whether the author had mistaken profanity for punctuation.

A period? No, apparently another swear word will do.

A comma? Better throw in another one.

And while we're at it, why not misuse God's name a few more times?

Project Hail Mary was considerably better in that department, although the underlying worldview still bothered me.

And now we have Exoplanet.

Different writer, familiar frustrations.

Here's a revolutionary suggestion for the publishing industry:

Try writing an exciting story without filling it with garbage.

I know. Radical thinking.

Perhaps somebody should apply for a research grant.

Andy Weir: The Man Who Apparently Believes Humanity Can Fix Everything Except Humanity

Now, before anybody gets upset, let me say something positive about Andy Weir.

He has an extraordinary imagination.

There is no question about it.

He knows how to help create fascinating stories about people confronting seemingly impossible situations.

But I have a serious problem with the worldview I see throughout his work.

From The Martian to Project Hail Mary, there seems to be a recurring message:

Give mankind enough scientific knowledge, enough determination, and enough time, and apparently there is nothing he cannot accomplish.

Stranded on Mars?

No problem. Bring a calculator.

Facing an extraordinary problem in outer space?

Don't worry. Somebody took chemistry in high school.

Civilization facing disaster?

Fear not! Humanity has a plan.

And apparently, somewhere along the way, God has become entirely unnecessary.

Well, isn't that convenient?

Of course, there's one rather small difficulty with this philosophy.

Humanity cannot even solve the problem of humanity.

We have spent thousands of years developing civilizations, advancing technology, and discovering increasingly sophisticated ways to accomplish remarkable things.

And what have we discovered?

We still lie.

We still steal.

We still hate.

We still go to war.

We still die.

But don't worry! Apparently, mankind is well on his way to conquering the universe.

He just can't conquer his own sinful heart.

The Bible gives us a rather different assessment of human ability.

Jeremiah 17:9 reminds us that the heart is deceitful above all things and desperately wicked.

And Proverbs 3:5 tells us not to lean upon our own understanding.

That is quite a contrast to the almost unlimited confidence in human ingenuity that I see reflected in Weir's storytelling.

I also strongly disagree with the evolutionary worldview I associate with his work.

I find it remarkable that someone can possess such a wonderful imagination and yet, in my judgment, place so much confidence in an explanation of life that leaves out the Creator.

Perhaps that is the irony.

A man with an imagination capable of inventing extraordinary fictional worlds seems perfectly comfortable with a worldview that, to my mind, gives mankind far too much credit and God far too little.

Now, to be fair, Benjamin Percy wrote Exoplanet, while Andy Weir served as executive producer. I am not suggesting that Weir personally wrote every line of dialogue or made every creative decision.

My criticism of Weir comes from the broader themes I have noticed in his work, particularly The Martian and Project Hail Mary.

And here's the frustrating part.

I can recognize his talent while strongly disagreeing with his worldview.

An author can be gifted and still be profoundly mistaken about the most important questions in life.

Would I Recommend This to Christians?

Well, here's where things get interesting.

Could I listen to Exoplanet again?

Probably.

Would I recommend it to my Christian friends?

No.

Would I go out of my way to tell people about it?

Not a chance.

In fact, I would generally recommend that Christians find something better to spend their time listening to.

Now, before somebody accuses me of trying to establish the First Reformed Church of Approved Audiobooks, let me make something clear.

I'm not making rules for everybody else's conscience.

If you're a Christian and believe you can listen to this kind of material without compromising your convictions, that's a decision you must make before God.

But as for me, I wouldn't recommend it.

And here's the final insult.

I listened to this audiobook for free.

Had I paid for it, I would have wanted my money back.

Imagine that.

The actors did an excellent job.

The sound engineers did an excellent job.

The story kept me interested.

And somehow, after all of that, I still wouldn't want to pay for it.

That's quite an achievement.

Final Verdict: Wonderful Sound Effects, Interesting Story, and a Refund I Thankfully Didn't Need

Exoplanet is one of those frustrating productions that demonstrates how much talent can go into creating something I ultimately cannot recommend.

The acting was excellent.

The sound effects were excellent.

The story was interesting.

The mystery and survival elements had genuine potential.

But the story felt rushed, the ending was underwhelming, and the unnecessary profanity seriously damaged my enjoyment.

And the broader confidence in humanity's supposed ability to overcome almost anything remains something I find deeply troubling in this kind of science fiction.

I cannot bring myself to give this audiobook a numerical rating.

How exactly does one rate a production that was interesting enough to listen to again but objectionable enough that I would have requested a refund had I paid for it?

Perhaps somebody at NASA can work out that equation.

In the meantime, I have a suggestion for Christian publishers.

Take the production talent demonstrated by audiobooks like Exoplanet.

Find some of the extraordinary true accounts from Christian history.

Bring those stories to life with full casts, excellent sound effects, and thoughtful storytelling.

And for goodness' sake, leave the profanity out.

We already have enough remarkable true stories to fill libraries.

We don't need to invent another universe to discover that God is sovereign over this one.

— Old Time Preaching
