BIBLICAL ARCHAEOLOGY · EARLY CHRISTIANITY

Buried in Ash: Did Christians Live in Pompeii Before AD 79?

The forgotten Roman city, the footsteps of the Apostle Paul, and an ancient inscription that may preserve one of the earliest references to Christians.

OLD TIME PREACHING · NOVEMBER 15, 2026

Imagine walking through the streets of Pompeii nearly two thousand years ago.

The sound of iron-rimmed cartwheels echoes over the stone pavement. A baker is preparing another day's bread. Merchants are opening their shops, neighbours are calling to one another, and the smell of cooking fires drifts through the narrow streets.

Beyond the city rises Mount Vesuvius.

To the people of Pompeii, the mountain is part of the familiar landscape. They do not know that their city is living on borrowed time.

It is the first century after the birth of Jesus Christ. The Roman Empire stretches from Britain to the Middle East. Caesar's authority reaches into nearly every corner of the Mediterranean world.

And somewhere in this world, a new message is spreading.

A Jewish man named Jesus of Nazareth has been crucified under Pontius Pilate. His followers proclaim that He has risen from the dead. They insist that salvation is found not through the gods of Rome, but through Jesus Christ alone.

Some Romans laugh at the message. Others despise it. Still others believe.

But did that message reach Pompeii before the city disappeared beneath volcanic ash in AD 79?

An extraordinary discovery may help us answer that question.

The Apostle Paul Arrives on the Bay of Naples

To understand the possibility of Christians living in Pompeii, we must first open the Bible.

In Acts 28:13–14, Luke records a remarkable event.

"And from thence we fetched a compass, and came to Rhegium: and after one day the south wind blew, and we came the next day to Puteoli: Where we found brethren, and were desired to tarry with them seven days: and so we went toward Rome."

Paul was travelling to Rome as a prisoner.

He had survived a terrible storm and a shipwreck. He had spent months making his way across the Mediterranean under Roman custody.

Now, around AD 60, he arrived at Puteoli, an important Roman port on the Bay of Naples.

And what did he find?

Christians.

Luke tells us that Paul found brethren there, and they welcomed him for seven days.

Think about that.

The gospel had already reached this busy Italian port before Paul's arrival. There were believers living, working, meeting, and worshipping in the region.

Puteoli was not Pompeii. They were different towns on the same bay. But the presence of Christians at Puteoli makes the possibility of believers in nearby Pompeii historically reasonable.

Could a merchant have carried the gospel from one town to the other?

Could a sailor have heard the gospel at Puteoli and spoken of Christ while visiting Pompeii?

Could someone in Pompeii have travelled to Puteoli and returned as a believer?

We cannot prove that any particular conversation occurred. Yet these are perfectly reasonable possibilities in a Roman world connected by roads, ships, trade, and human relationships.

The gospel did not spread through grand cathedrals or imperial decrees. It spread as ordinary believers carried the message of Christ from place to place.

And the Bible tells us that those believers were already on the Bay of Naples.

A Roman House and a Story from the Old Testament

Now imagine leaving the crowded streets and stepping into a house in Pompeii.

The building is known today as the House of the Physician, or the House of the Judgment of Solomon.

Its ancient decorations included something astonishing: a painted scene that appears to depict a story from the Old Testament.

In 1 Kings 3:16–28, two women appear before King Solomon, each claiming to be the mother of the same child.

Solomon proposes dividing the living child with a sword. The true mother begs that her baby be spared, revealing her identity through her compassion.

It is a story about wisdom, justice, motherhood, and the ability to discern truth.

And a scene resembling that biblical account was painted on a wall in pagan Pompeii.

The original fresco is now preserved in the National Archaeological Museum of Naples.

What makes this so interesting is not that it proves Christians owned the house. It does not.

The painting reflects a biblical story, but we cannot be certain of the homeowner's religion or why the scene was chosen. Roman artists sometimes borrowed stories and imagery from cultures beyond their own.

Nevertheless, it demonstrates that a story associated with the Hebrew Scriptures was known in the artistic world of Pompeii before the eruption.

And that brings us to a question.

If stories from the Old Testament could reach a Roman household, could the message of the promised Messiah have reached the same city?

The New Testament tells us that the earliest Christians proclaimed Jesus as the fulfilment of the Scriptures.

Paul himself reasoned with people from the Scriptures, explaining that the Christ must suffer and rise again.

See Acts 17:2–3.

The connection is worth considering, though the fresco alone cannot establish the presence of Christians.

A Bakery That Might Not Have Been Christian

Bread was an essential part of daily life in Pompeii.

Archaeologists have uncovered ovens, mills, and bakeries throughout the city. Some still contain the equipment used by the people who worked there.

In one bakery, a mark on the wall attracted considerable attention.

It resembled a cross.

For a time, the building was associated with the possibility of Christian occupants, earning the name of a Christian bakery.

Imagine what such a discovery would mean.

A Christian baker working in Pompeii while the apostles were still alive. A man or woman earning a living under Roman rule while confessing Jesus Christ as Lord.

It would be a remarkable window into first-century Christianity.

Unfortunately, archaeology does not always give us the answers we hope to find.

Researchers have suggested that the supposed cross was actually a representation of baking equipment rather than a Christian symbol.

The mark remains disputed, and it cannot confidently identify the bakery's occupants as Christians.

This is where Christians must be careful.

We should never force the archaeological evidence to say something it does not say.

The Bible does not need an incorrectly identified cross in an ancient bakery to establish its authority.

Nevertheless, the search for early Christian evidence in Pompeii does not end there.

In fact, one of the most interesting discoveries involves something far less impressive than a painting or a stone cross.

It involves words scratched onto a wall.

The Writing on the Wall: Someone Mentions Christians

Picture a traveller staying in a Roman lodging house.

Perhaps he has come to Pompeii on business. Perhaps he is passing through the town on his way somewhere else.

We do not know his name, his occupation, or why he was there.

But someone in Pompeii left writing that may have referred to Christians.

In 1862, during excavations of the ancient city, archaeologists recorded an inscription that has since become the subject of serious scholarly debate.

The inscription is generally catalogued as CIL IV 679.

The most important part appears to contain a form of the Latin word Christianos or Christiani — Christians.

If this reading is correct, the inscription was written before AD 79, because the eruption buried the city.

That would make it one of the earliest surviving archaeological references to Christians.

Think about what that means.

Long before Constantine, medieval churches, or the religious institutions that later dominated Europe, someone may have written the name of Christians on an ordinary wall in a Roman town.

Not in a church.

Not in a carefully preserved theological manuscript.

On a wall.

Exactly the sort of place where ordinary Romans wrote jokes, insults, advertisements, and personal remarks.

Some scholars have understood the writing as mocking Christians. Others disagree about the surrounding words and what the inscription originally meant.

We cannot confidently claim that its full message was "Christians, those cruel haters," as some interpretations suggest.

The inscription itself deteriorated after excavation, and scholars have had to rely substantially on the records made by nineteenth-century investigators.

Yet the question has not been forgotten.

In 2024, biblical scholar John Granger Cook published a study in New Testament Studies, arguing that there are good reasons to believe that the inscription probably contained the word Christians.

That is a significant historical finding, although it does not settle every question.

If Cook is correct, the word Christians was being used in Pompeii during the same century in which the New Testament was written.

And Scripture tells us that this name was already familiar to the followers of Jesus.

Acts 11:26 declares:

"And the disciples were called Christians first in Antioch."

The term did not begin with the Roman Catholic Church, Constantine, or some later religious movement.

It belonged to the earliest generations of believers.

The Pompeii inscription, if correctly identified, gives us a possible glimpse of how that name appeared in everyday Roman life.

What the Romans Thought of Christians

Being called a Christian in the first century was not necessarily a compliment.

The Roman world was filled with religious traditions, public ceremonies, household gods, and sacrifices associated with civic life.

Christians refused to worship those gods because they believed there was only one true God.

They proclaimed a crucified and risen Saviour.

To many Romans, that message sounded absurd.

The Apostle Paul understood this reaction.

In 1 Corinthians 1:18, he wrote:

"For the preaching of the cross is to them that perish foolishness; but unto us which are saved it is the power of God."

Imagine being one of those early believers.

Your neighbours may worship Jupiter, Apollo, Venus, or other Roman deities. Religious rituals are part of public and private life.

But you refuse to bow before those gods.

You believe that a Jewish man crucified under a Roman governor is the risen Lord of all.

Your faith may bring misunderstanding, ridicule, or hostility.

This was not an imaginary danger.

The New Testament repeatedly prepares Christians for suffering.

Peter wrote:

"Yet if any man suffer as a Christian, let him not be ashamed; but let him glorify God on this behalf."

— 1 Peter 4:16

The possibility that someone mocked Christians on a wall in Pompeii fits the broader atmosphere described by the New Testament.

It does not prove a particular persecution happened in Pompeii.

But it gives us a reason to imagine how the earliest followers of Jesus might have been regarded in an ordinary Roman town.

Another Inscription: "He Lives"

There is another mysterious piece of evidence.

In 1955, archaeologists discovered a small inscription in Pompeii that has been interpreted by some researchers as containing the Latin word vivit.

It means:

"He lives."

The markings also include lines that have been interpreted as a cross.

Now imagine the possibility.

Someone living in Pompeii might have placed a cross and a declaration of life together on a wall before AD 79.

For a Christian, those two ideas would have carried enormous meaning.

The cross was the instrument of Christ's suffering and death.

The resurrection was the declaration of His victory over death.

Paul wrote in Romans 6:9:

"Knowing that Christ being raised from the dead dieth no more; death hath no more dominion over him."

Could the inscription have been a declaration of faith in the risen Jesus?

Possibly.

But there is a problem.

The lettering and the supposed cross are uncertain. Scholars disagree about what the marks actually represent.

We cannot identify the author as a Christian, and we cannot confidently say that the inscription describes Christ's resurrection.

The possibility is fascinating. The proof remains incomplete.

Still, one can understand why such a small inscription has attracted attention.

If it were Christian, it would place an expression of resurrection faith in Pompeii during the lifetime of the earliest Christian generations.

The Day Pompeii Disappeared

Then came AD 79.

For generations, the people of Pompeii had lived beneath Mount Vesuvius.

They built houses, raised families, bought and sold goods, attended public entertainments, and went about their ordinary business.

Then the mountain erupted.

A towering column of volcanic material rose into the sky. Pumice and ash fell upon the city. Buildings were damaged and roofs collapsed beneath the weight.

As the disaster continued, deadly surges of hot gas, ash, and volcanic debris swept across the region.

People fled.

Others sought shelter.

Many did not survive.

The eruption buried Pompeii and nearby settlements beneath volcanic deposits, leaving streets, buildings, objects, and traces of ordinary lives preserved for centuries.

The exact calendar date of the eruption remains debated, but the year AD 79 is firmly established.

And beneath all that ash were the possible traces of a faith that was only beginning to spread throughout the Roman Empire.

Did a Christian family die that day?

Did someone who had heard the preaching of Paul perish beneath the volcanic debris?

Was there a believer in Pompeii who had once worshipped alongside the Christians at Puteoli?

We do not know.

We should not invent individuals and present them as historical facts.

But we do know from the Bible that followers of Jesus lived nearby, and we have archaeological evidence that may place knowledge of Christians inside Pompeii itself.

That alone is remarkable.

A City That Perished — and a Gospel That Continued

There is something deeply sobering about walking through Pompeii today.

The buildings remain.

The streets remain.

The ovens, paintings, and household objects remain.

But the people are gone.

For centuries, their daily lives lay buried beneath the ash of Vesuvius.

The Apostle James reminds us:

"For what is your life? It is even a vapour, that appeareth for a little time, and then vanisheth away."

— James 4:14

Pompeii is an extraordinary illustration of how quickly human life can change.

Yet there is another part of this story.

The Roman Empire that once ruled the Mediterranean eventually fell in the West. The city of Pompeii was buried. Generations passed into history.

But the gospel preached by the apostles continued to spread.

The message carried by ordinary believers, announced by Paul, and proclaimed throughout the New Testament has not disappeared.

It is the same message of repentance toward God and faith in our Lord Jesus Christ.

See Acts 20:21.

And it is the same gospel that declares:

"For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast."

— Ephesians 2:8–9

Archaeology cannot save a soul.

It cannot prove every claim of Christianity by uncovering a scratched wall or an ancient painting.

But it can help us understand the real historical world in which the gospel was proclaimed.

The first Christians were not imaginary characters in a religious legend.

They were people living in actual towns and cities, travelling on Roman roads, working in shops, gathering in homes, and confessing Jesus Christ in a world that often rejected their message.

And perhaps, beneath the ash of Pompeii, we have found a faint trace of their presence.

Not a grand monument.

Not a cathedral.

Not an imperial decree.

Just a few ancient letters on a Roman wall.

Letters that may have carried the name Christians almost two thousand years ago.

And that is a story worth remembering.
